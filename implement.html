Triangle is designed to be implemented, not just admired.

Start small. Pick one decision area. Form one triangle. Track one month of decisions. Learn. Expand.

Step 1: Form Your First Triangle

Who Should Be in the Triangle?

For executive decisions:

  • CEO/Executive Director
  • Operations lead
  • Finance/Strategy lead

For board decisions:

  • Board chair
  • One subject-matter expert
  • One independent voice (not aligned with either)

For project decisions:

  • Project lead
  • Technical expert
  • Stakeholder representative

What to Avoid

  • Dyads — Two people collapse into conflict without a tiebreaker
  • Large committees — Five+ people diffuse accountability
  • Homogeneous triangles — Three people with identical expertise miss blind spots

Triangle Design Checklist

  • Three people, distinct perspectives
  • Clear decision domain (what this triangle decides)
  • Meeting rhythm established (weekly? per-decision?)
  • Decision log created (shared, accessible)
  • AI advisory access configured (if applicable)

Step 2: Establish Decision Rules

The Consensus-First Voting Rule

  1. Facilitator aims for genuine consensus — extract information and disagreement from everyone. Not agreeing for the sake of it.
  2. When consensus fails, 2/3 carries — A simple majority within the triangle
  3. If facilitator is in the minority — they must represent the majority. Not override it.
  4. Dissent is recorded — The minority view is logged with the decision

Facilitator Selection

  1. Volunteer — Anyone who wants to be facilitator volunteers
  2. Everyone votes — No self-voting allowed
  3. Tiebreaker — External authority decides, or surrender to chance (coin flip)
  4. Recorded — When tiebreaker used, it is logged publicly

The Decision Map

Decisions are not flat. Some depend on others. The Map makes the sequence visible.

  1. Nodes — Each decision is a node on the Map
  2. Arrows — Show dependencies between decisions
  3. No loops — Forward only. Circular dependencies are flagged by AI
  4. Parallel — Some decisions can happen simultaneously

Lock & Forward

  1. Complete all four steps → decision is locked
  2. No going back — The team moves to the next decision
  3. Challenge path — Two non-originating triangles can reopen a lock
  4. Authorized override — CEO or equivalent managing the system can reopen unilaterally, with full public logging

Decision Requests

When Team B needs a decision from Team A before they can proceed:

  1. Team B sends request — becomes a node on Team A's Map
  2. Timer starts — adjustable in dashboard
  3. Team A completes four steps → locked → Team B notified
  4. Team A doesn't respond → timeout → "No Decision" lock → Team B notified

The Challenge Rule

  1. Two non-originating triangles must agree to challenge
  2. Decider is excluded from the challenge vote
  3. Biblical pattern — by the mouth of two or three witnesses
  4. Fractal — same threshold at every scale

Dynamic Authority Protocol

Authority shifts based on:

  1. Proximity to facts — Who is closest to the situation?
  2. Track record — Whose prior judgments proved accurate?
  3. Vindicated dissent — Whose past objections were proven right?
  4. Facilitator assignment — The person chosen to run the four-step process for this decision
Example: In a safety crisis, the field coordinator leads the decision — not the CFO, not the CEO. The triangle decides who has authority per decision.

AI Dependency Tool Protocol

  1. AI listens during meetings — Flags new decisions as they emerge
  2. Suggests dependencies — What the new decision depends on, what depends on it
  3. Auto-sequences — Suggests where the new decision fits in the Map
  4. Flags conflicts — Identifies circular dependencies and cross-team blockers
  5. Human confirmation required — AI advises, humans approve. Always.

Best Practices

  • Record dissent verbatim — Don't summarize. Preserve the actual reasoning.
  • Set follow-up dates — Every decision gets reviewed in 3-6 months.
  • Track who was right — This builds the track record for dynamic authority.
  • Make it accessible — New triangle members should read past decisions during onboarding.

Step 4: Review and Learn

Monthly Triangle Review

Once per month, the triangle reviews:

  • Decisions made — What did we decide?
  • Dissent patterns — Who dissented? Were they right?
  • Authority shifts — Did the right person lead each decision?
  • AI accuracy — When did AI recommend correctly? When did humans overrule wisely?

Quarterly Expansion

After one quarter of successful triangle operation:

  • Document what worked — What decision types fit the triangle model?
  • Identify gaps — What decisions are still solo? Why?
  • Form new triangles — Expand to adjacent decision domains
  • Train new members — Bring more people into the Triangle framework

Common Implementation Challenges

"This feels slower than one person deciding"

Response: It is — initially. But decision quality improves, and implementation is faster because dissent was heard upfront.

Mitigation: Start with high-stakes decisions only. Don't triangle every small choice.

"Recording dissent feels uncomfortable"

Response: Good. That means it's working. Discomfort means you're breaking the harmony-at-all-costs pattern.

Mitigation: Frame dissent as "institutional memory" not "obstruction." You're preserving intelligence for future triangles.

"AI recommendations feel authoritative"

Response: That's the risk. AI is advisory, not decisive. The triangle must consciously affirm or overrule.

Mitigation: Always record the AI recommendation separately from the human vote. Make the overrule explicit.

"Who decides when authority shifts?"

Response: The triangle decides — dynamically, per decision. This requires humility and honest track-record review.

Mitigation: Use the decision log. Data beats hierarchy. If someone's dissent has been vindicated three times, they lead the next related decision.
